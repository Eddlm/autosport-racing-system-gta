# Steering control — how this is actually done elsewhere

**Read this when** the request or the bug touches: steering controller, PID, P/I/D, pure pursuit, Stanley, aim point, lookahead, preview, gain, gain scheduling, feedforward, anti-windup, integral clamp, countersteer, opposite lock, oscillation, weaving, "why does it oscillate", "how do other games do it", "is a PID the right tool", **loop order, damping ratio, "why is it stable"**, standing error, straight-line drift, steering limiter, steer authority, **grip-normalised gain**, velocity-vector vs heading reference.

**Depth lives in `STEERING-CONTROLLER-SURVEY.md`** (36 implementations, 10 documented disagreements, every claim carrying its source URL, inaccessible sources listed rather than papered over). This file is the distillation; the survey is the evidence. ARS comparisons here are mine and go stale — **the code wins**.

## ⚠️ STATUS — the PID / measured-yaw-rate law in this file was tried and ABANDONED

**Everything below about a P/I/D law applied to the *aim error*, and about a correction reading the *measured yaw rate*, records a design that was built, driven through many variants, and then rolled back.** The live steering system is the **pre-PID authority chain, restored from `1f4516d`** and simplified since (`cd6efea`): **pure pursuit** on the bearing to the aim point (gain 2, clamped at 90°) + a side-by-side heading correction + a damper whose reference is that same aim's curvature + the countersteer blend, then the steer limiter and the slew. There is no course-error term, no off-track recovery term and no outer PD, and the rival walls bound the *aim*, not the steer (`Racer.cs:492`).

**Four claims below are directly superseded:** the live **course** error is **velocity**-referenced (not nose); there is **no PID**, no `SteerI` and no `SteerD`/`SteerTrim` — the steering knob is `SteerDampingGain`, an **absolute gain in seconds** scaled by 25 over the forward speed and capped at 1, so the dial *is* the gain below 25 m/s (`97cbc34`, cap `5bb94bd`, driver-verified; the grip-normalised `SteerDampingScale` retired in `a9603c4`); the damper runs `(yaw − target) × gain` with the target read from the **aim point's** required yaw (`SteerDampingAimReference` is true, the zero reference kept one flip away for A/B rather than deleted); and the steerable ceiling's Bias/Slip dials are gone — the **yaw-usage turn-in share** that replaced them was itself retired, leaving one governor: the peak-slip cap, geometry only as its fallback, with the slide `Math.Max` and the countersteer and damper side raises (`d434b86` added the second governor, `cd6efea` removed it). The section below describes the **live** chain; where it keeps the rejected design, the paragraph is marked as history.

**The lessons worth carrying forward** (they are why this section is kept): a reference that contains its own correction closes a measurement loop through its own input; a veto that **drops** rather than **clamps** turns a monotonic dial into a cliff; `v·tan(δ)/L` is a fiction once the tyres leave their linear range; and the field's "read yaw rate directly instead of differentiating" advice is sound in principle but **did not beat a well-tuned PD chain in this game**.

## What the restored chain actually is, and where its stability comes from

**This section reads the live chain as a control loop; the design it replaced is kept only where a paragraph is marked as history.**

**The analysis of the live law.** Reduced to the terms that are always on, `ComputeSteering` is:

```
courseDir = v̂                                                    // Racer.cs:508
aim       = steerRefPoint.Position + steerRight × aimLane         // Racer.cs:531-539
δ_lane    = atan(PursuitGain · sin(bearing) · WheelBase / range)  // Racer.cs:695, PursuitGain :682
δ         = steerKP·(δ_lane + δ_sideBySide) + δ_damper            // Racer.cs:595, :605, steerKP :577
δ_damper  = −gain·(yawRate − yawTarget)                           // Racer.cs:590, :596
δ        += (δ_countersteer − δ) × slidePriority                  // Racer.cs:611-612
```

**There is no course-error term, no recovery term and no `laneBias` term.** The lane becomes a steer by pure pursuit and pursuit is the loop's only position law; rival repulsion is *added* to that lane steer rather than replacing it (`Racer.cs:566`), and the side-by-side match is the heading difference between the two cars' forward vectors (`Racer.cs:665`).

**Despite its name, `_debugLaneAimPoint` is the pursuit's input, not a debug ornament.** The point is built at `Racer.cs:531-539` and handed straight to `PursuitSteerDegrees` at `Racer.cs:543`, where the law is an `atan` on the bearing from the car's travel direction to it (`Racer.cs:695`, `:707`) — so the live chain does carry a geometric gain and an aim angle, and the lane becomes a steer here and nowhere else. The bearing is clamped at `MaxPursuitBearingDegrees` so `sin` cannot fade past abeam (`Racer.cs:684`, `:694`), and inside `SmallAimBearingDegrees` with no slide the command is zeroed outright (`Racer.cs:687`, `:617`). The aim's lateral coordinate also carries the Gs-aware lead — half the lateral motion the car is already committing to (`Racer.cs:535`, blend `:133`), on by default (`AutosportRacingSystem.cs:189`) — which is zero at steady state.

**The stability is loop *order*, not the setpoint: pursuit is position feedback driving a plant whose input is a rate, and the damper is the loop's only rate feedback.** The bearing carries the lateral error, so to first order the pursuit demands `2·WheelBase·lateral/range²` — proportional on *where the car is*, not on where it points — while the plant answers a steer with a yaw rate (`Racer.cs:695`, `:707`). That is a second-order loop, and nothing but the damper closes it (`Racer.cs:596`, `:602-603`).

- **The position gain schedules itself with `range`, and `range` is a preview *time*.** `LookAhead.SteerRef` is `speed × SteerPreviewSeconds` clamped to `8`–`30` m (`Racer.cs:2670`, `:129-131`), so inside that band the same lateral error asks for less steer the faster the car goes — the loop's gain falls with speed without a gain schedule being written anywhere.
- **There is no integrator anywhere, and no curvature feedforward either.** Nothing integrates against the command `ApplySteerLimits` caps, so the anti-windup item below is moot while there is no integral (`Racer.cs:1120`); the literature's standing-error job falls to the pursuit instead, because it acts on **position** — a car offset from its aim carries a bearing back to it and converges rather than settling at a permanent offset.
- **Saturation, not algebra, is what the fast case meets.** `sin` caps what a swinging aim may ask for (`Racer.cs:684`), and the limiter closes the command last on the peak-slip cap, with corner geometry only as its fallback (`Racer.cs:1045`) — the reason a limit corner's steer must also carry the slip angle that generates the force, which is the quantity `TRLateralAtSpeed` feeds `PeakSlipCeilingAt` (`Racer.cs:1014`, `:1035`). Past the tyres' linear range `v·tan(δ)/L` is a fiction, and there the damper, not the proportional term, is what restrains the loop (`Racer.cs:955-956`).
- **Rejected design, kept as history.** The abandoned PID law put P on the *aim error* with **no position term at all**, so its standing line error could only be fought by raising P — the "P 1.0 swerves / P 0.5 is stable but lazy" trade — and an I term would have added 90° of phase lag to manufacture the ring it was meant to remove. The lever for "lazy" was never P: the live lane's one gain is `PursuitGain` (`Racer.cs:682`), and the dial that looks like a steering gain is the damper's (`Racer.cs:970`).

**Two properties of the heading reference, usually mis-stated:**

1. **The reference is the velocity vector, but it is the bearing's origin, not a course error** (`courseDir = Car.Velocity.Normalized`, `Racer.cs:508`). The pursuit measures the aim's bearing from the car's *travel* direction (`Racer.cs:707`), so a body rotated hard against its own velocity reads as perfectly tracked — the slide-blindness that made this reference worth keeping.
2. **The preview is a constant *time*, clamped in distance, and grip never enters it.** `LookAhead.SteerRef` is `speed × SteerPreviewSeconds` clamped to `8`–`30` m (`Racer.cs:2670`, `:129-131`), so the time is 0.5 s only inside that band: below the floor it stretches, above the cap it shortens. Nothing in the steer reference or the aim reads grip, so no part of the live lane law is grip-scheduled.

**The straight-line regime, and why an offset on it still converges.** With no corner line and no avoidance, `targetLane` is whatever `ComputeHighSpeedLane` returns, and it returns zero unless the node `speed × HighSpeedLaneChordSeconds` ahead carries a curve radius under `HighSpeedLaneRadiusMeters` (`Racer.cs:718`, `:722`, `:729`, `:674`); where it does fire it aims at the **inside edge**, `−cornerDir × (roadWide − half the car)` (`Racer.cs:731-737`). So on a straight with no rivals the aim is the **track centre** — `ApplyRivalWalls` clamps a zero between walls that sit exactly at `±roadWide` when nothing constrains them (`Racer.cs:884`, `:922-923`, `:938`) — and the lane steer is the pursuit bearing to that point: **zero only when the car is already on the aim**, and a real correction for a car holding a lateral offset. The pursuit is not gated either: it runs whenever `TryGetSteerContext` resolves (`Racer.cs:496`, `:543`, `:624-633`), so a straight is lane work, not a regime where the lane law is switched off.

**One body-yaw feedback path, and it is the loop's only rate term.** The signal is the raw native yaw rate — `GET_ENTITY_ROTATION_VELOCITY.Z` in °/s, unfiltered and undifferentiated (`Racer.cs:3591`) — and the term is `−gain·(yawRate − yawTarget)`, where `yawTarget` is the yaw the **aim point** requires, `fwdSpeed × _steerAimCurvature`, dropped above `LateralTractionCurve × SlidingFraction` because a sliding car's wanted rotation is the countersteer's (`Racer.cs:589-590`, `:714`, `:942`). The gain is `SteerDampingFor` — the dial scaled by speed and divided by mechanical grip (`Racer.cs:966-971`) — and the term never adds lock: one that agrees in sign with the rest of the command is zeroed, and one that crosses neutral keeps `DamperCrossingShare` of its overshoot (`Racer.cs:598`, `:602-603`). `steerKP` is unity and dimensionless, so the pursuit and the side-by-side correction enter at 1.0 against that gain (`Racer.cs:577`, `:595`), and there is no course feedback anywhere in the sum.

**Rejected design, kept as history — the zero-reference damper, and the toll it paid.** Until the term damped the *excess over* the yaw the aim requires, it damped against zero, so in a steady corner it removed `gain × ω` of command the corner needed; the loop paid that as a **standing lateral offset from its target lane**, growing with speed and corner tightness, which is the mechanism behind "stable but a little lazy". **Re-referencing removes the setpoint error without weakening the damping**: `yawTarget` does not depend on `yawRate`, so `∂/∂ω` of `−gain·(ω − yawTarget)` is exactly `−gain`, and only the setpoint moves. The durable trap that build exposed is a convention, not a number: a reference built in the steer command's own frame (positive = left) must **not** be negated, or the damper doubles its opposition in corners instead of removing the toll.

- **Removing the damper is not the fix for that toll — driven, and the term is load-bearing.** With `SteerDampingEnabled` off the gain is zero and the sum falls back to the pursuit plus the side-by-side match (`Racer.cs:957`, `:970`, `:595`), and the observed behaviour is that the cars **cannot hold centre**: a limit cycle around zero steer — steer in, overshoot, steer back. Deleting the term leaves position feedback with no rate feedback at all on a rate plant, which is why it reads as stabilisation rather than trim (`Racer.cs:955-956`).

**Two couplings and one sign test on a raw signal.** The limiter's countersteer decision is `Math.Sign(requestedSteer) != Math.Sign(VehicleData.YawRotationPerSecondDegrees)` — a sign test on the unfiltered native read, so a car dithering across zero yaw flickers it, and `Math.Sign(0)` matches nothing (`Racer.cs:1086`, `:3591`). The damper is not private to the sum either: the limiter reads `_damperTermDeg` to decide whether the answering side may run to full lock (`Racer.cs:1114-1118`), and the slide blend starts from the damper-inclusive command while its countersteer target carries no damper at all, so moving the damper moves where the blend starts (`Racer.cs:605`, `:611-612`).

- **Rejected design, kept as history — the track-curvature reference.** A damper referenced to the track's own required yaw (`v·κ`) was built and then deleted, along with its switch and the steer variable it fed; what it left behind is the convention trap above. Its cost estimate also flattered the zero form: "a corner only needs `L/R` of steer" counts the kinematic angle alone, while a limit corner must also carry the slip angle that generates the force — the quantity the limiter's ceiling is built from (`PeakSlipCeilingAt` on `TRLateralAtSpeed`, `Racer.cs:1035`, `:1045`).

**The gain is grip-normalised, and speed-scheduled above the reference speed.** `SteerDampingFor` is `SteerDampingGain × speedScale / max(BaseMechanicalGrip, 1)`, with the divisor read from the base mechanical grip rather than the live perceived one (`Racer.cs:970`, `:3554`), so at a fixed speed the menu dial is a **uniform multiplier** — every car's term moves by the same ratio — and the divisor is floored, so a low grip reading cannot blow the term up. The divisor is the grip itself rather than its square root, so the term falls faster with grip than a constant-damping-ratio law would need, and `speedScale = min(1, SteerDampingReferenceMps / |v|)` means the term follows the steer a yaw rate actually needs above that reference speed while the dial *is* the gain at and below it (`Racer.cs:969`, `:961-965`). Speed therefore enters the live loop twice, through this scale and through the pursuit's growing `range`.

## The dominant architecture is feedforward + feedback, not a lone PID

Every serious implementation surveyed splits the steering command into an open-loop **feedforward** term and a closed-loop **feedback** term — a two-degree-of-freedom architecture, explicitly named as such in the racing literature:

```
delta = delta_feedforward(curvature of the line ahead)  +  delta_feedback(PID on cross-track error)
```

- [Control-Theoretic PID Steering](https://www.emergentmind.com/topics/control-theoretic-steering-pid) states the law outright: `delta(t) = delta_pp(t) + delta_pid(t)`, feedforward geometric (pure-pursuit) term plus summed PID feedback.
- [Speedgoat, autonomous racing](https://www.speedgoat.com/Portals/0/adam/Content/0weSCmFk8U6yqmpJ_9cnug/Text/Sensor%20Fusion%20and%20Motion%20Control%20for%20Autonomous%20Racing%20Cars.pdf): "All controllers are based upon a two degrees of freedom architecture, separating the control request generation into a feedforward part and a feedback part".
- [Stanford, Kapania ch.2 "Feedforward-Feedback Steering Controller"](https://ddl.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj25996/files/media/file/2016_thesis_kapania_0.pdf); [TUM's feedforward benchmark](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.21111).
- The richest shipped example is **Ziggy Racer** (Forza Horizon 6, virtual gamepad, ~71 Hz): `steer = k_ff·κ·load_comp + k_head·α + PID(cross-track) + countersteer(sideslip)`.

**Why the feedforward exists:** a feedback-only law must carry a *standing error* to produce a steady command, so P alone settles a permanent few degrees of error — a permanent lane offset or corner cut. The literature's first fix is the feedforward; the integrator is the fallback.

## Pure pursuit, stated properly

Pure pursuit is **itself a proportional controller**: the [Sensors 2025 pure-pursuit paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11820862/) gives the gain as `2/l_d²` on lateral error (so `2L/l_d²` in steering-angle terms), equivalently `(2L/l_d)·α` on the aim angle. The [autonomous-driving-book](https://github.com/YangyangFu/autonomous-driving-book/blob/main/book/3-trajectory-tracking/lateral-control/pure-pursuit.md) notes it "can be tuned at different speeds by creating a relationship between the speed and the lookahead distance."

**ARS tried the geometry form — `atan(2 × wheelbase × sin(error) / aimDistance)`, trimmed by `SteerTrim` — in the abandoned PID law, and does not use it now.** The live chain (see the STATUS banner) pursues the lane with **pure pursuit** on the aim point's bearing, plus a side-by-side heading correction, a damper referenced to that aim's curvature and the slide blend. **The pure-pursuit gain lesson still holds** and is worth keeping: `sin` rather than the raw angle saturates the demand as the aim point swings abeam instead of asking for full lock, and the gain self-schedules only because the aim distance moves with speed. **Two repair-policy lessons from that era, still load-bearing:** the repair pass **snaps an in-list numeric key to the nearest offer** rather than resetting it, so an off-list stored value becomes a permanent and *invisible* error; and **every declared default must be a member of its own domain list**, or the repair rewrites it on the next launch. And `SettingsRepair` **prunes any declared-then-undeclared key** — that is the mechanism that retires a key from every existing ini, which is how `SteerTrim` / `SteerD` / `SteerI` went when the PID was abandoned and how `SteerKD` came back — renamed to `SteerDampingScale` for the grip-normalised era, and to `SteerDampingGain` when the dial became the gain directly.

## What is universal is *preview* scheduling, not gain scheduling

**Correction to an earlier claim in this file.** "Gain scheduling by speed is universal" is too strong. Most implementations **never touch the gain** — TORCS `berniw`/`bt`/`damned`, USR default and Assetto Corsa hold a flat `1/steerLock` or per-car `STEER_GAIN` — and the *effective* gain moves only because the **aim distance** moves. Explicit gain scheduling is the minority, and it contradicts itself in direction:

- **More gain at low speed**: Game AI Pro ch.40 — "at low speed the vehicle may require **much larger** K values".
- **Less authority at high speed**: Unity Standard Assets (`maxSteerAngle` 28° → 23%), Unity's own tutorial (`steeringRange` 30° → 10°), NwliZz, and ForzaETH's `k_speed`. ForzaETH even ships **both** an upscale and a downscale in the same code, silently fighting each other.
- **A band-pass**: USR `SteerMod` reduces gain both above target speed and below it, with the reasons stated verbatim ("stop bouncing over curbs"; "avoiding or have just recovered from avoiding").

**So ARS's arrangement — flat trim × a gain derived from a speed-scaled preview — is the dominant pattern, not an oddity.**

**The one real derivation, and it is about the preview:** ForzaETH's stability criterion is that the guidance loop's natural frequency `ω_n = √2·v/L_d` (with `τ = L_d/v`) must stay below half the vehicle-plus-**delay** bandwidth. As `L_d` grows, `ω_n` falls — so **`L_d` must grow with speed** to hold the loop inside the bandwidth. That is the proper justification for a speed-affine preview: not comfort, but delay margin.

**Four sources name the hazard of scheduling at all** — Game AI Pro ch.40: "if the coupling is too great, a hidden positive feedback loop can be set up, resulting in instability". Ziggy Racer hit exactly that: feeding the *planner's own* curvature into the steering feedforward "created a planner ↔ tracker limit cycle … the two rang together into a bang-bang steering oscillation." **Never close the loop through a signal the loop itself produced.**

## The reference vector: body forward dominates; ARS's velocity reference is near-unique

| Reference | Count | Who |
|---|---|---|
| **Body forward / heading** | 13 | TORCS berniw/bt/tutorial, Unreal RacingAI, Unity CarAIControl + AIVehicleRoutingBuddy, Unreal TrafficAI, TUM example PP (body frame), ForzaETH MAP **shipped code**, ForzaETH PP, Game AI Pro ch.39, and others |
| **Velocity vector** | 3 | TUM countersteer (`atan2(vy, max(1,vx))`), TUM ESC (`beta = atan2(vy, vx)`), Ziggy Racer (sideslip catch) |
| **Track tangent** | 2 | TORCS bt `speedangle` (**throttle gate only**), TORCS SCR `angle` sensor |

**Load-bearing consequences:**

- **The velocity-referenced aim error is genuinely rare, and where it appears it is a stability layer, not a path-following error.** ARS uses it as the primary error. **Nobody in the corpus does that** — so this design has essentially no precedent to lean on, in either direction. Not a bug; an original choice whose behaviour ARS has to establish empirically. Note what it buys and what it does not: referencing **velocity** rather than the nose makes the error a pure *course* measure (which is why it needs no separate heading term), but it still says nothing about slip angle — ARS keeps that in the separate countersteer blend, and so does the field.
- **Do not cite ForzaETH/MAP code as precedent for it.** The MAP ICRA paper specifies the velocity vector; ForzaETH's stack paper and its shipped code use heading. The same group contradicts itself across its own publications.
- **The track tangent is never the steering error** in any source — it appears only on throttle gates. ARS's `speedangle`-style velocity-vs-tangent quantity is, in TORCS bt, a *throttle* input: the same primitive on a different channel.

## Look-ahead: speed-affine nearly everywhere, grip nowhere

16+ sources scale preview with speed; one with **track width** (SuperTuxKart's corridor search); one with **driver skill** (simplix). **Not one ties preview to grip or friction** — including stacks that have a full Pacejka model or per-wheel slip estimates available. ARS's `speed / grip × leadScale` is the only grip-scaled preview in the corpus.

**This is the one real open technical question about ARS's existing steering geometry.** The ForzaETH derivation is a genuine argument against grip: `ω_n = √2·v/L_d` is checked against a bandwidth that **does not contain grip**, so scaling `L_d` by grip moves `ω_n` without moving the constraint. But that is a *stability* criterion, and low grip genuinely does need an earlier turn-in for the same curvature — a *path-tracking* criterion, and the two are different things. No source resolves it.

**ARS has an in-game data point no source replicates**: the grip trend was already found to drive corner cutting, which is why the full `1/grip` was replaced by a compressed manual `grip→leadScale`. **The experiment that would settle it: A/B `speed/grip` against a speed-only preview, measured on cross-track RMS and steer-reversal count rather than by feel.** That is the highest-value experiment this research identifies.

## Treat the preview as a state, not a memoryless function

TORCS `bt` and `damned` both floor the preview against `oldlookahead − v·dt` — a **rate limit on the preview itself**, described as preventing "snap back" of lookahead on harsh braking. It is the only such guard in the corpus, and it matters because it makes the preview a state with dynamics.

**This is the field's answer to a hazard ARS has**: the lane systems are a **hard switch** (outside → inside, no lerp) and `targetLane` can step, which steps the **lane error**, and so the command. **The old amplification is gone** — the D term that turned that step into a one-frame spike differentiated the aim error, while the live damper reads yaw against the aim's own curvature (`yaw − fwdSpeed·_steerAimCurvature`), which a lane step *does* move — so the step reaches the wheel as a jump in the pursuit and the damper together, ramped off by the slew rate at a fixed °/s rather than spiking in a single frame. **Rate-limiting `targetLane` still fixes it at the source, and is still the better fix**: it removes the step from the P term instead of relying on the downstream slew to hide it, and it survives a slew-rate retune.

## Anti-windup is mandatory when the actuator saturates — and ARS's steering saturates

Only **five** surveyed sources carry an integral term, and **four of them guard it**. **ARS has none today** — the live chain is P and D only — so read this as the prerequisite for turning an I on, not as a description of the code. (The abandoned PID law's integrator *was* unguarded: unbounded, integrating unconditionally, while `ApplySteerLimits` caps the command to a small allowance at speed — so enabling it wound the integrator against a cap it could not overcome.)

Documented remedies, all cheap: **clamp** the integral (Ziggy: `cte_int` ±3; TUM: ±1325 on both PIDs) and **suppress integration while the actuator is saturated** (Ziggy's steer-clip anti-windup bleeds the integrator when the wheel is pinned at full lock and still winding). Game AI Pro ch.40 names both and adds a third: a **leaky rolling integral** — each frame reduce the integral by `(1−T)%` and add `T%` of the current error — which needs no saturation signal and self-heals. ARS already has `_steerLimitedThisFrame` for the saturation gate.

## Damping belongs on yaw rate, not on a D term over the path error

**Almost every implementation that needed damping put it on yaw rate or curvature, not on a D term**: TORCS berniw/bt (a yaw-rate error term, gain 0.1), Ziggy (a *filtered* yaw-rate comparison, `r_des = v·κ`), TUM PP and Eelis03 (low-pass curvature filters), Unity CarAIControl (a yaw-rate speed-caution term), NwliZz (a yaw-acceleration stabiliser).

Game AI Pro ch.40 warns about the alternative in the same breath as recommending it: "If the input data (R) is noisy, the derivative term can fluctuate in an undesirable manner… **this is effectively adding another integrator.**"

**Three consequences for ARS — all three were acted on *inside the abandoned PID law*, and this block is the record of a rejected design and of what it taught, not a description of the code (see the STATUS banner):**

- Our D differentiates the aim error, and that error is measured from the car's nose to the aim point (body forward since `6f4dc45`) with a raw per-tick divide, so it is noisy. The field's answer is not to smooth it but to **read yaw rate directly** — and **that is what the abandoned law did** (`GET_ENTITY_ROTATION_VELOCITY.Z`, a native read), so in it the filter and the numerical derivative were both gone. (The restored chain reads yaw rate only as a **damping input** to the PD: `− SteerDamping × yawRate` — **against zero**, so the raw-`ω` warning below describes the live code rather than a hypothetical; the analysis at the top of this file carries the cost.) USR's cheapest trick is to fold the damping into the aim *bearing* rather than add a term: `targetAngle − (yaw + yaw_rate/15)`. The old D was mathematically a yaw-rate term *plus the aim point's own swing* (`ė = θ̇_aim − ω`, so the D term carries both `−D·v/L·δ` and `+D·θ̇_aim`), so writing it explicitly keeps the behaviour and drops the numerical differentiation and the aim-point half at once. **It does not drop the speed mis-scheduling**: the closed loop with a yaw-rate term `−Kw·ω` is damped by the identical `1/(1 + Kw·v/L)` factor, so a fixed gain still over-damps at speed and under-damps at walking pace — contamination/noise and scheduling are two separate defects needing two separate fixes, and the scaling is the second. **The whole speed story was one identity: `D/P = SteerD·v/L`** — the flat-gain form this replaced — at any error and any grip; equivalently, D's lead time over the loop's own response time, which put the crossover at `v = L/SteerD` (D stopped being a trim and became the dominant term above it, around 60 mph with the old gain). **That law kept the speed-invariance through the geometry gain** (`Kd = SteerTrim × SteerD × 2L/d`), giving `D/P = 2·SteerTrim·SteerD·grip/leadScale` — speed-invariant, which was the property worth having and the reason the `2L/d` scaling survived its own rework. Its correction was against the rotation the *command* asked for (`ω_des = v·tan(δ_ff)/L`), not against zero — a steady corner gave `ω = ω_des` and therefore **zero correction**. That reference is load-bearing: against raw `ω` the term would reduce steer permanently in every corner, because a steady corner has `e/ω = d/(2v)` — 0.2–0.4 s at *every* speed and radius, inside any sane threshold. **The ideal-model estimate is a floor, not a target**: exact instantaneous cancellation is `SteerD ≈ leadScale/(2·grip)` (≈0.20–0.40 across the grip range), but the driver confirmed **0.50** works best in game. That is the *same* pattern the old lead-time form showed — the modelled cancellation point was weaker than what the car actually needed — so treat any derived gain here as a lower bound to start above, and do not "correct" a working value down to a computed one. Using `2L/d` rather than `1/speed` is deliberate: `aimDistance` already has a floor, so the gain self-limits at low speed instead of running away at rest, which matters for the stuck-recovery crawl. The driver confirmed the flat gain was too weak in slow corners *before* this fix, which is the evidence the identity was right.
- **ARS's disabled inner yaw loop was the field's answer, badly implemented — and it was never live.** The *shape* (a yaw-rate error term) is what TORCS berniw has shipped since 2002 (gain 0.1); what was wrong in ours was the expected-yaw reference, derived from the *applied* steer and hence meaningless once the car slides — not the choice of signal. (The abandoned PID law later used the **commanded** feedforward for exactly that reason: it stays well defined in a slide because it never reads what the car did.)
- **The gate D needs is itself a symptom of the contaminated input.** Because `ė = θ̇_aim − ω` carries the aim point's swing, D could add lock when the *request* moved while the car had done nothing — the aim point is pinned to a 1 m node, so `θ̇_aim` steps at every crossing. Gating on the yaw rate leaked twice: steer and yaw disagree during a slide (exactly the countersteer case), which let the additive correction through, and `Math.Sign(0)` matches nothing, so a straight (yaw ≈ 0, or dithering across it) held the gate open. The gate then compared against `pSteer`, making the correction strictly unwind-only — honest, but it also meant D supplied no lead at all. (The `I` that was meant to trim a steady-state following error could not either: the same gate zeroed it.) Reading yaw rate directly (item 1) made a two-sided correction possible, **and the additive half was then vetoed by choice** — so that law went back to unwind-only, but with the veto as a **direction decision, not a guard against a contaminated input**. It was stated on the **magnitude** rather than on matching signs, because a sign test cannot fire when `pSteer` is exactly 0 and a nonzero correction there would add lock out of nothing.
- **Driver-observed, and structural rather than tuning: a high `SteerD` reads as a brief damping burst at the *end* of the steer, not sustained pacing — the car steers at full aggression and is only damped in its final moments.** Three effects stack. (1) The veto drops the additive half, so the term is silent while the car is still *building* rotation on turn-in and can only appear once the car rotates faster than commanded. (2) `ω_des = v·tan(pSteer)/L` **collapses as the steering demand falls** — approaching straight, `pSteer → 0`, so `ω_des → 0` — while the car's actual rotation **lags** it, and that lag is exactly what manufactures the positive excess at the unwind/exit phase. So the term's window *is* the last moments of the steer. (3) The correction kills its own trigger: paying back the excess lowers `ω`, which removes the excess, which removes the correction — so the **higher** the gain, the **shorter** the burst it produces. Net: a large `SteerD` gives full-aggression turn-in and a sharp damped arrival (the driver's stated preference), while a small one stays active longer and reads as general pacing instead. **Do not "fix" the burstiness by clamping the correction** — the brevity is the mechanism working, not a defect.
- **The reference can be either the raw geometric command or the speed-limited one, and in normal driving the two are nearly the same number — verified, and it explains more than it seems.** The variant that references the **post-limit, pre-slew** steer (`_limitedSteerDeg`, captured at the end of `ApplySteerLimits` and read one frame later because the limiter runs *after* `ComputeSteering` and `TranslateSteerToInput` overwrites its output in the same tick) was built and driven: **behaviour is very similar to the raw reference.** The reason is arithmetic, not coincidence — the live ceiling is vanilla's **grip-blind** speed curve, ~20° at 41 mph and ~15° at 60 mph on a 40° lock, while the pure-pursuit command in a normal corner is only **~1.5°** (30 m/s, 100 m radius, `d ≈ 15 m`, `e ≈ 4.3°`) and the geometry keeps it small as speed rises because `d` grows with speed. So the limiter **rarely clamps**, and the two references are the same value most of the time. **This means the earlier prediction that "D goes blind at speed, because the limiter prevents the car reaching `ω_des`" was largely untested by normal driving** — the blindness needs the limiter to actually be biting. The TRlat-form ceiling this replaced *did* scale with grip, and on a low-grip car its ceiling was the same order as the command itself; the vanilla ceiling is grip-blind, so that divergence case is gone and only the abeam-aim-point transient remains. **Test the difference there, not on a grippy car.** Reverting to the raw reference is one line (substitute `pSteer` in the yaw-rate computation); **the veto tests `pSteer` either way, deliberately**, so the reference is the only changed variable.
- **The veto must CLAMP at centre, not drop — dropping made D non-monotonic, and that is why `SteerD` 1.0 felt the same as 0.5** (found by the driver, then fixed). The first form discarded any correction that would take the command past zero (`if (|pSteer + correction| > |pSteer|) correction = 0`), so a larger `SteerD` asked for larger cuts, crossed that threshold more often, and therefore did **less** work: the dose response was linear, then a cliff to zero. With `pSteer ≈ 1.55°` (a normal 100 m corner at 30 m/s) the cliff sat at ~8.6 °/s of excess for `SteerD` 0.5 but only ~4.3 °/s for 1.0 — so **any meaningful overrotation put both settings past the cliff, and both contributed nothing.** That is exactly the reported symptom: the two settings were indistinguishable. That law's final form clamped instead: `reduction = correction * pSteer < 0 ? min(|correction|, |pSteer|) : 0`, so more gain meant more damping, saturating at wheels-straight. **The ceiling is structural, not a tuning limit**: the most such a term can ever do is *neutralise* the lock, because it may never steer the other way — the price of unwind-only — and above saturation every gain value looks identical again, so **tune in the mid-range, never at the extremes.** The product test also covers `pSteer == 0` exactly, where a sign comparison cannot fire. **General lesson worth keeping: a "never exceed" rule must clamp, not veto — a veto turns a monotonic dial into a cliff and inverts the top of its range.**

## Course error and slip angle are different states — and which the loop sees depends on its reference vector

**Every survey claim here is about the field, and stays true; only the "ARS" half of each contrast moved.** The **restored chain's course error is velocity-referenced** (course-over-ground), which is the *blind* one below; the **nose-referenced variant was the abandoned PID law's**, and it was that law that was A/B'd and dropped in favour of velocity before the whole thing was abandoned in turn. Both are kept because the difference between them is the durable insight. With `θ` the direction to the aim point, `ψ` the heading, `v` the velocity heading and `SlideAngle = ψ − v`:

| reference | error | on a slide |
|---|---|---|
| **velocity** (course-over-ground) | `e_vel = θ − v` | a **course** error only. For a pure body rotation with the course intact, `e_vel = 0` — the car reads as *perfectly tracked while it is sideways*, and only the blend countersteers. |
| **body forward** (textbook pure pursuit) | `e_nose = θ − ψ` | the slide **directly**, since `e_nose = e_vel − SlideAngle`. A slide *subtracts* from the error, so the law **countersteers on its own** — the right sign, but see the magnitude trap below. |

- A car pointed 20° off while tracking straight produces a course error, and **either** reference corrects it by steering. Ordinary.
- A car **sliding with its velocity vector still pointing at the aim point**: the velocity reference reads zero error and does nothing; the nose reference reads the full slip as error and counters it. That is *why* 13 of ~20 surveyed implementations are nose-referenced, and why nose-referenced controllers so often need no separate slide term at all.

**Neither reference *reads* slip — `e_nose` merely contains it.** The blend via `VehicleData.SlideAngle` remains the only explicit consumer of that state.

**ARS runs two layers, and how they relate depends entirely on which reference is selected:**

- The live course-error P chain in `ComputeSteering` → the line, from whichever reference is active.
- The **slide countersteer blend** → **slip**, via `VehicleData.SlideAngle`, ramping in against TRlat and **overriding** the path command at full priority. It is the only consumer of slip in the entire steering path.

With the **velocity** reference the two layers are **complementary** — course vs slip, the field's shape, and the honest justification for the blend getting the last word.

### The magnitude trap: the sign is right, the gain is ~5× too small

**Do not conclude from the table above that a slip-containing error gives you countersteer worth having.** That error passes through the pure-pursuit gain `2L/aimDistance` ≈ **0.225° of steer per degree of error** at 30 m/s, so a genuine 10° slide yields `atan(2 × 2.7 × sin 10° / 24)` ≈ **2.2° of opposite lock** — right sign, invisible magnitude, well inside the slew and the limiter. The velocity reference has the same gain and the *wrong* sign (in a developed slide it commands *into* the corner).

**And in a corner the nose-referenced error does not even reach zero.** The aim point sits at the chord angle `c ≈ ld/(2R)` off the tangent (~6.9° at 30 m/s in a 100 m corner, more in tight ones) while the nose sits at the slip angle β, so `e_nose = c − β` stays **positive** until the slide exceeds the chord angle. Below that the nose reference merely **unwinds** steer as the body rotates — and that is self-reinforcing: less steer means less yaw, which means a *smaller* β, which keeps `|SlideAngle|` under the blend's ramp onset (~`0.3 × TRlat`) and leaves the blend contributing nothing.

**So the blend is not redundant with the nose reference — it is the same signal at roughly 5× the gain**, and it is why the reference choice barely affected slide recovery: at a 10° slide the nose-referenced PID offered ~2.2° and the blend ~6.2°, and at full ramp the blend *replaces* the command outright. **The reference choice is a line-tracking decision, not a slide-handling one.** Making the path term a real slide participant would need the slip term entering with a gain near 1, not routed through `2L/ld`. Note also that **ARS has no understeer detector or sideslip model anywhere** — the blend's bare `|SlideAngle|` magnitude gate is the only thing in the steering path that looks at slip, so nothing "classifies" a car as understeering; it merely fails to trip the gate.

**The countersteer magnitude has a physical neutral value, and it is 1.** A steer angle equal to the slip angle points the front wheels along the **velocity vector**, which removes the front tyres' contribution to the rotation — that *neutralises* a slide. Any multiple above 1 points the wheels *past* the velocity vector and actively rotates the nose back, which is a deliberate over-correction rather than a stabilisation. ARS *exposed* it as the `SlideCountersteer` knob for exactly this reason — it is a taste dial around a derived value, not an arbitrary gain — **but that knob is now gone**: the blend answers a share of the slide angle that ramps from half to all of it (`CountersteerShare`), and the limiter raises that side's limit to `min(|SlideAngle| × blend weight, lock)`, so the countersteer can reach the velocity vector but not pass it. Two bounds agree with the physics: the share's top at 1, and the grant at the slide angle.

That is TUM's architecture — a path-tracking controller with a slip-gated countersteer layer on top (`δ += k·(α_f − α_r)`, gated on `|α_r| > |α_f|`). **The survey mis-characterised ARS as folding the slide into the path error; it does not.** The two terms are not competing for the same job, so their interaction is a *priority* choice (save the car first), not redundancy — which is the honest justification for the blend getting the last word.

**Two consequences to keep in mind:** while the blend holds full priority, **course tracking is suspended**; and a slide that never develops enough slip to trip the ramp is invisible to the blend, so only the PD chain's indirect response to it exists.

## Slides: the field mostly does nothing; ARS is ahead of it

**The survey's strongest negative result.** Of ~20 implementations, **17 do nothing about slides at all**; only **three** control them (TUM's countersteer — the only real published law, `δ += k·(α_f − α_r)` gated on `|α_r| > |α_f|` — plus Ziggy's yaw-rate + sideslip detectors and USR's `getAvoidSteer`); the rest avoid the problem by giving the AI extra grip (Assetto Corsa's 20% overhead "so the AI's weird and twitchy inputs don't make them spin", AMS2, rF2's steering-lock multiplier), deleting the slide kinematically (Unreal TrafficAI rewrites heading from wheelbase geometry), or penalising it in a reward (GT Sophy).

**Correction to an earlier claim in this file.** I recorded the oversteer-countersteer / understeer-slow-down split as an "independent confirmation" of ARS's design. It is one project (Ziggy), not a norm. What *is* common is the **understeer half**: three sources measure sideslip and respond by **slowing down rather than steering** — the ESC idiom with the countersteer half simply absent. So ARS's asymmetry (oversteer gets the wheel, understeer gets the speed pipeline) is a minority position that one strong source shares.

**ARS's steering limit no longer has a countersteer exemption — and that closes the gap this section used to record.** USR reaches the intent by making the clamp *wider* under rear skid; ARS now does the same thing explicitly, raising only that side's limit (and only up to the slide angle), so two independent codebases converge on *the limit must widen for the correction that saves you, without disappearing for it*.

**ARS's live configuration is therefore the field's shape, not a deviation**: a course controller for the line, plus a slip-gated countersteer layer with priority over it, plus a limiter that widens on the countersteer side instead of exempting it. The piece still missing relative to TUM is that TUM reads **per-wheel slip angles** (`α_f`, `α_r`) and gates on the *rear* exceeding the *front*, while ARS reads a single body-level `SlideAngle` — so ARS cannot distinguish front-axle from rear-axle slip, and its gate is on slip magnitude alone rather than on the front/rear imbalance that actually defines oversteer.

## Speed-dependent steering limits are normal — but check how hard they bite

TORCS's own **human** driver ships a "steer speed sensitivity… the factor of reduction of steering values when the speed grows. the default value is 1.0 which corresponds to **half the steering at 360 km/h**" — a mild, feel-oriented reduction on the input path, structurally the same idea as ARS's `ApplySteerLimits` but far gentler than ARS's allowance.

Ziggy's headline diagnosis is the cautionary tale: it burned a long campaign trying steering-law levers against a corner where the wheel was **already correctly saturated** — "you cannot fix a saturation by asking for more of a resource that is exhausted." The fix was reducing demand (a speed cut in the approach), not raising authority.

**Diagnostic for ARS:** if cars run wide in a corner *identically at every gain*, the limiter's allowance is the binding constraint, not the controller — measure commanded steer against the allowance before touching gains.

**On the slew rate — read the code for its value; the design question is whether it should exist at all.** Exactly one shipped racing game publishes a steering rate limit, and its stated reason is **comfort** ("to reduce shaking of karts", because "otherwise the karts counter-steer to fast") — while Eelis03 documents a **0.6 rad/s (34°/s)** actuator limit *causing* a limit cycle. So 34°/s is the number to measure any rate limit against, and a rate limit can be the oscillation *source* rather than a cure. ARS's rate should be justified on stability grounds, not comfort. Note also that ARS's **faster countersteer path was a guess, never validated in game, and has since been removed** — the slew is a single rate, so a slide correction now arrives no faster than any other steer.

## Run the integral in distance, not in time

"The integrator AND the derivative gain need to be scaled by the speed. Normally the PID updates every millisecond but what you really want is to update the PID as a function of distance travelled or rate of speed." Integrating `error × dt` makes the integral speed-dependent for free; `error × ds` removes that. ARS already adopted time-sampled discipline for the Gs calculator; the steering loop has the same argument available in distance terms — **moot today, since there is no integral to sample** (see the analysis above), and live again the moment one is added. Note the *derivative* half is already half-answered: the loop's time constant is `L/v`, and the live damper's gain carries the reciprocal above its reference speed — `SteerDampingFor` scales the dial by `min(1, 25/|v|)` over the grip floor, so the term is flat **below** `SteerDampingReferenceMps` and weakens as speed rises above it (`Racer.cs:969`).

## Reading constants out of this literature

- **Code beats papers.** Three documented traps: TUM's paper and code disagree on the **sign** of the countersteer (porting the paper steers *into* the slide); TUM's code defaults and shipped YAML disagree in the dangerous direction (`max_brake_pressure` is declared **twice in one file**); and one repo's README contradicts its own YAML.
- **Cite the right publication.** MAP's paper and MAP's code disagree on the reference vector (above).
- **Cross-track error as the *primary* steering signal appears in exactly one shipped default in the whole corpus** (simplix), and there its authority is capped at **15%** of total steer. TORCS berniw caps its lateral-error correction at 0.1 for the same reason. **External support for ARS's split**: the lane systems decide *where* to aim, the pursuit law carries the authority, lateral error is a bounded trim.

## The lane pursuit is ungated, and a straight-line offset converges

`ComputeSteering` calls the pursuit whenever `TryGetSteerContext` passes: the old guard on the *target lane* went with the error-based lane-bias era (`dcef913`), so a straight or gentle sweep — where `ComputeHighSpeedLane` returns zero — still steers on a real bearing rather than on nothing.

**What the aim is decides the rest, and two earlier notes in this file had it backwards.** The aim offset is `targetLane` (`Racer.cs` `aimLane = targetLane`): with no corner line, no avoidance and a high-speed line that reads zero inside its radius gate, `targetLane` is `ApplyRivalWalls(0, roadWide)` — the **centre**. A laterally displaced car therefore carries a bearing to the centre line ahead and is steered back onto it, so a straight-line standing offset **converges rather than being held**. The car's own offset (`carOffset`) is read in exactly one place, the optional Gs-aware preview, whose shift is zero at steady state — it never becomes the aim. **The gate removal still has not had its own drive.**

## Where ARS sits, and the ranked list

ARS runs **pure pursuit** on the aim point's bearing under a unity `steerKP`, a side-by-side heading correction, **a yaw-rate damper referenced to that aim's own curvature**, and a saturating countersteer-exempt limit. No integral, no curvature feedforward. That is the field's feedforward-plus-feedback *intent* reached by a different route — the pursuit's position term does the standing-error job the curvature feedforward usually does — with two unusual choices (the bearing is measured from the **velocity** vector, so the loop is slide-blind by construction; nothing but the damper's divisor is grip-scheduled) and a damper whose gain is flat below its reference speed and falls as `1/|v|` above it (`97cbc34`, cap `5bb94bd`, driver-verified). Ranked by expected payoff:

1. **The yaw damper's *reference* and *gain* were both repaired, then both reverted (`a9603c4`) — two open defects again.** The signal half stays done: the live damper reads measured yaw rate directly, with no numerical differentiation and no aim-point swing, as `(yaw − target) × gain`. The reference is **settled, and no longer zero**: the live damper targets the yaw the **aim point** requires (`SteerDampingAimReference`, `Racer.cs:960`, fed by `_steerAimCurvature` at `:714`), the track form (`f8304f9`, `r_des = v·κ`) and its `SteerDampingTrackReference` switch are deleted, and centring is driver-confirmed with it. Damping against **zero** charged the permanent toll of `gain × ω` in every corner as a standing lane offset (see the analysis at the top of this file), and the drive that preferred zero predates the course error's deletion, so it was confounded. The gain side's speed tracking returned uncapped (`97cbc34`) and is now **capped at 1** — the dial is the gain below 25 m/s — which the drive confirms improved the system (`5bb94bd`). Grip normalisation (`SteerDampingScale / grip`) stays retired; the grip divisor does that job. If the toll ever shows up again in the lane line, the reference is the lever that removes it.
2. **Clamp and gate an integral** before I is ever turned up — **a precondition, not a task**: there is no integral to clamp today. Prefer clamp + saturation gate, or Game AI Pro ch.40's leaky rolling integral.
3. **Rate-limit `targetLane`** so a hard lane switch cannot step the lane error — the TORCS preview-rate guard, applied to the lateral target. Removes the step from P at the source rather than leaning on the downstream slew to absorb it.
4. ~~A/B the grip-scaled preview against a speed-only one~~ — **WITHDRAWN: the premise does not exist.** The steer reference is `speed × SteerPreviewSeconds` clamped to `8`–`30` m (`Racer.cs:2670`, `:129-131`), and grip enters neither the reference nor the aim, so there is no grip-scaled preview to A/B and the constant-time arithmetic this item rested on described a form the code never had. What is still testable is the **band**, not the grip: outside it the preview time stretches below the floor and shortens above the cap, so the live question is whether those two bounds should move.
5. **Add the curvature feedforward** (`wheelbase × curvature`) if a standing aim error shows up as a permanent lane offset. **Partly answered already**: while engaged, the live cross-track P does null an offset, because it acts on position rather than course — so the job the feedforward exists to do is currently done by the lane term. Revisit only if the lane term's authority is deliberately reduced, or if a *straight-line* standing offset ever matters — it converges today, since the ungated pursuit aims at the centre (see the ungated-pursuit section above).
6. **Sharpen the slide layer's input.** The blend is live again, so this is no longer "reinstate slide handling" but "give it a better signal": TUM gates on the **front/rear slip-angle imbalance** (`|α_r| > |α_f|` — the actual definition of oversteer) while ARS gates on the magnitude of a single body-level `SlideAngle` against TRlat, which cannot tell a rear-axle slide from a front-axle one. Per-wheel slip is available in the handling struct's wheel data if it is worth the plumbing.
7. Consider a distance-based integral, a lookahead fallback (PX4: target the closest point on the path when the lateral error exceeds the lookahead) and error-adaptive aggression (JPL/Kelly: let the gain grow with lateral error) — the last two as honest upgrades to the **live** off-track recovery term, which is a severity-scaled push-back once past the safe edge, not a lookahead fallback.

## The live pipeline, the lane laws, the speed plan and the corner lifecycle

Moved whole out of the project `AGENTS.md` to keep the instruction chain inside its cap. The order and the invariants stay in the parent; the systems live here.

## Per-frame pipeline (`Racer`)
1. `UpdateTrackPosition` (`Racer.cs:2606`) — local node search with a global rescan fallback, time-based lookaheads, route radii, lap ticks.
2. `ComputeTargetSpeed` (`Racer.cs:1453`) — builds the intended speed; see the Speed pipeline.
3. `ComputeSteering` (`Racer.cs:492`) — resolves the lane into a target offset and sums the authority chain into `Control.SteerDegrees`. **`_debugLaneAimPoint` is the lane law's own input** — the bearing from the car's travel direction to that point is what becomes the lane steer, so it feeds the error rather than only the debug line.
4. `ApplySteerLimits` (`Racer.cs` `ApplySteerLimits`) — the two side limits closed by a single `Clamp`, and it runs **last among the writers of the steering request**. Its governing quantity is the **slide**, and since `473` it is the only one: the ceiling is the peak-slip cap, the corner geometry being only a fallback when the peak reads unusable, raised by the maneuvering ramp below 30 mph; the slide then **adds** to it rather than replacing it, and the answering side is raised by the countersteer allowance. **The retired yaw-usage governor is the cautionary tale**: it scaled the ceiling by the car's own yaw rate, so a car not yet rotating got about a fifth of the law — least authority at exactly the moment it needed most — and under understeer, where yaw is low *because* the steering is not working, it took steering away from the car it exists to rescue. Yaw rate also cannot tell a clean turn-in from a stepped-out rear, and the slide's sign can. **A steering writer added below it is a silent bypass** — the bug class this shape removes. The **slip-balance knee is deleted** (`d434b86`).
5. `ConvertSpeedToPedals` (`Racer.cs:1199`) — one `[-1,1]` combined input, split, then the **reason-cap compose** (also pre-slew) and the slew. **GridWait lives in three places** (`ComputeTargetSpeed`, `ProcessAI` handbrake pulses, `ComputeSteering` hold-straight) — keep them in sync.
6. `TranslateSteerToInput` (`Racer.cs:1433`) — slews steer toward target at `SteerSlewRate`, then remaps degrees → −1..1.

## The steering authority chain (`ComputeSteering`)
**The aim point is the whole geometry**: the lane steer is pure pursuit — the bearing from the car's travel direction to the aim point, its curvature `2 sin(bearing)/range` times the wheelbase (`PursuitSteerFromBearing` `Racer.cs`), with pure pursuit's factor of two carried in `PursuitGain`. The **course error is deleted**: referenced to the road's direction at the lookahead node it over-commanded by roughly `lookahead/radius`, which *was* the corner cutting, and the bearing already carries that information. **Off-track recovery is deleted**: off-track it and the lane call were the same law at the same point at gain 1 each, so it doubled the command rather than recovering anything distinct, and the lane pursuit alone recovers the car — driver-verified, and it can oscillate because the car keeps enough speed to overshoot. Then rival repulsion **added** to the lane steer and never overwriting it, the side-by-side heading match (**one-way** — only while the overlapping rival moves toward this car's side, `Racer.cs:662`), and the **yaw damper referenced to the aim point's required yaw** — the loop's only rate feedback, so **it is load-bearing: with it off the cars limit-cycle around centre**, and against *zero* it taxed every steady corner into a standing lateral offset.

**No term is gated** — the old `|targetLane|` guard is gone, so the pursuit runs whenever `TryGetSteerContext` passes; `ApplySteerLimits` and the slew rate only cap the output. Full analysis, and why the aim-error PID was tried, driven and **abandoned** in favour of this chain: `AGENTS-STEERING.md`.

**The slide batch hands the countersteer to a blend and leaves the damper only a share of its crossing (`d5a643f`, re-cut in `7297050`)**: the blend's weight ramps to full across a quarter to half of the **static** `LateralTractionCurve` — not the speed-scaled peak, and with no rear-led sign gate — and the target answers a share of the slide angle that itself ramps from half to all of it across half to the whole peak (`Racer.cs` `CountersteerShare`). The damper only ever subtracts — a term that would add steer in the sign the command already has is zeroed, so a left command never gets more left from it — and keeps half its authority past neutral, where it is countersteering rather than merely less into the turn (`a0134d9`); the axle gate that used to guard that halving, and `UndersteerDegrees` with it, are deleted. The weight is computed before the damper because the limiter's allowance reads it, and the old gate constant is renamed `SlidingFraction`, whose only consumer is now the damper's reference gate (`Racer.cs:589`).

## Lane systems — where on the track the car aims
Resolved in `ComputeSteering`, later overriding earlier: **high-speed line** (`Racer.cs` `ComputeHighSpeedLane`, runs every tick and takes the inside edge only where the node a lookahead ahead carries a radius under the gate), **corner line** (`Racer.cs` `ComputeCornerTargetLane`, the distance-parameterised ideal line), **avoidance** (`Racer.cs` `ComputeAvoidAheadLane`), then **`ApplyRivalWalls`** (`Racer.cs`) clamps the result between track bounds and rival walls.

**Lane systems only *set* the target lane; how hard it is pursued is the authority chain's job.** Every lane target insets by half the car's width — the inside line, the avoidance bound and the corner line alike; `OutOfTrackDistance` (`Racer.cs:2560`) is centre-based on the raw half width, which is why a car pushed off is judged by its centre.

- **Two steering lookaheads, only one of them named — do not conflate them**: `ComputeHighSpeedLane`'s chord is anonymous, while `LookAhead.SteerRef` — which feeds every lane bound, the aim point and the debug line — is a **preview time** (`SteerPreviewSeconds` `Racer.cs`), floored and capped in metres, rather than a fixed distance, because a fixed distance raises the loop's natural frequency with speed while the actuator lag stays put. `SteerLookaheadMinMeters` is its floor and is **live** again.
- **The corner line is a distance-parameterised offset profile from the apex** (`ComputeCornerTargetLane` `Racer.cs:762`, `IdealLineOffset` `Racer.cs:753`), so grip never enters the lane path: a low-grip car needs a lower speed, not an earlier turn-in. Its lead is the geometric one stretched by a constant multiple, and it now spans the entry only — the exit blend and its heading step are gone with the exit half.
- **The corner line is entry-only (`d5a643f`)** — the exit half and the passed/active-apex bookkeeping are deleted, so past the apex the lane comes from the next corner's entry profile or the high-speed line, and its offset applies only while the car has asked to position and is above apex speed. Adjacent profiles still merge by taking the further-in of the two, and an opposite-direction pair is still excluded as a chicane.
- **A sweeping corner suppresses its own corner line through the positioning decision**: the flag requires a tight corner (`Racer.cs:2853`) and route speed governs a sweeper's speed either way. That regime is no longer switchable — the Route Speed Limit toggle went in `70e177c` — so the lever that shifts it is **Route Offset (mph)**.
- **Corner-commit maneuvers** replace the corner line with a lane beside the target rival on the corner's inside, and clean up when their apex is passed.
- **The lane pursuit was gated on the target's offset, not the car's error, and the gate is now REMOVED** — the pursuit runs whenever `TryGetSteerContext` passes, so a straight or gentle sweep (where `ComputeHighSpeedLane` returns zero) now drives the pursuit from the car's live position, aiming at the centre, instead of getting no lane steer at all. The guard was a leftover of the error-based lane-bias era, when "guidance" meant a lane request or a rival wall (`dcef913`); the gate removal has **not** had its own drive.
- **A lane becomes a steer by pure pursuit off its aim point** — the bearing from the car's travel direction to `steerRefPoint.Position + steerRight * targetLane`, turned into an angle by the wheelbase (`PursuitSteerDegrees` `Racer.cs`); **no radius produces a steer**, and every lane source feeds it. The inside line aims at the **inside edge**, and the steer carries pure pursuit's factor of two (`PursuitGain`, `Racer.cs`); a target that steps from wherever the car is instead holds a constant error and never converges. The ordering is load-bearing — car's-heading-to-the-aim is the steering command's own convention, and a minus inverts the lane. **Do not "fix" an aggressive lane move with a ceiling**: a ceiling flattens any lane law into a constant-command phase that builds lateral velocity the short tail cannot shed, and that *is* the overshoot — the lookahead's length and the step into the line are the levers (`AGENTS-TECHNOTES.md`). **The aim offset carries a Gs-aware preview**: its lateral coordinate shifts by half the car's projected lateral motion (`ProjectAhead` `Racer.cs`), so the command leads the drift instead of reacting to it. It is zero at steady state, so it moves the lane-change transient and never the steady gain — and it therefore steers *earlier still* when the car is being pushed away from its target, which is the case that falsifies "it reels back aggression" as a general claim. **The aim offset is clamped to the drivable edge** (raw half-width less half the car, not the raw edge the rival walls bound to), because the preview shift is applied after the wall clamp and could otherwise aim the car off the track. **With no lane demand the aim is the centre line**, so a straight-line offset converges rather than being held, and that same aim is what turns an off-track car back; the preview only damps the drift the correction gives up rather than bounding it.

## Speed pipeline — how fast the car goes
`Intention.Speed` is `min(cornerSpd + Corner Offset, followTrackSpd + Route Offset)`, then clamped by `MaxSpeed` and the off-track projection cap. **The offsets are split, one per authority, and each is applied inside the `min`** — so Corner Offset also lifts apex-limited speed while apex speed itself stays pure physics (`Racer.cs:1544`). `Brain.CurrentIntention.CorneringSpeedLimit` is computed alongside the offsets and **read nowhere**, not even by the HUD (`Racer.cs`) — a known write-only field, so a plan that expects it to bite is wrong.

- **`cornerSpd`** — the kinematic braking plan toward the apex speed over the distance to the apex node (`Racer.cs:3030`). Its decel (`Racer.cs:3072`) is a grip-limited base **plus the gravity component of the mean grade over the braking span**, sampled along the same trimmed span the solve integrates. **A crest before the corner is in that decel too**: `CornerPoint` carries a crest node, its curvature at a fixed probe and the extent its unload ramps over (`AssignCrestNodes` `TrackLoader.cs`), the solve scales **only the grip-limited base** by the clipped-triangle unload (`CrestDecelFactor` `Racer.cs`) because vertical curvature is a normal-load effect while grade is along-slope gravity, and the entrance walks back so the plan starts while the car is loaded. **Its target is the crest's entry — where the track stops being flat and the vertical curvature begins — not the unload's peak (decided, not built):** braking from the peak spends the ramp already unloaded, so the entry is the last point at which the car is still fully loaded, and this exists so a car never arrives airborne with nothing to brake with.
- **Gs and degrees are not interchangeable, and a threshold censors its own evidence.** `HillGripDeltaGs` returns speed-scaled Gs (`v²κ/g`) while a pitch break is pure geometry, so one feature reads a couple of degrees and a couple of G depending on unit and window — a launch test must name the speed it assumes. A scan that stops at its threshold can never log what sits below it, so "the distribution shows a clean gap" is not evidence the threshold is safe; log the unthresholded value first.
- **Never smooth on top of smoothing, and state baselines in metres.** `TrackPoint.Elevation` is already a ±3-node central difference of the route (`TrackLoader.cs:208`) and `Direction` a ±2-node read, while the crest curvature reads the raw positions over ±6 — two vertical reads of the same road through different filters, so building curvature by differencing `Elevation` would compound the filter and shift the onset it is meant to find. The reads carry an unstated ladder (`ExactRadius` ±2, `PreciseCurveRadius` ±4, `GeneralCurveRadius` ±10, crest ±6); it wants one convention with two named rungs — short to locate a feature, long to measure its strength — counted in metres, because spacing runs 0.88–2.39 m per node and a node count is a different filter on every track (`AGENTS-BACKLOG.md`).
- **`followTrackSpd`** — route curvature from a time-based lookahead window (`Racer.cs:2760` sampled, `Racer.cs:3140` the law), the authority for sweeping corners, and **always in the `min`**: the Route Speed Limit toggle that could drop it to 999 was removed in `70e177c`, replaced by the additive Route Offset. Its one live modifier is the **`SteerLimitedSpeed` blend** (`Racer.cs:1522`, 70% steer-implied speed to 30% route speed) and it is fed the *clamped* steer of the previous pass, so a tighter steer ceiling **raises** route speed.
- **Slope grip loss is LIVE** (exponential pitch model on corner + route speed) — ignore any older note claiming otherwise. The hill/crest grip floors are tuned as one knob; read the code for current values, and note the known issue that the model reads an upcoming hill and brakes before the car reaches it.
- **Pedal gain is one linear map, no feedforward** (`Racer.cs:1247`), with the brake side divided by `BrakeErrorMultiplier` on top because braking is deliberately the softer side. Both pedals then ramp to that target at a fixed slew rate.
- **Throttle limits are per-reason (`1ebbd2c`)**: every limiter owns a `MaxThrottleFrom<X>` level field in `UpdateThrottleReasonCaps` (`Racer.cs:1672`), and `ComposeThrottleCap` (`Racer.cs:1765`) takes `min(base, reasons)` **pre-slew**, naming the winner into `ThrottleReason` — the standing answer to "why isn't the pedal full", read off the bar sphere wearing the winner's colour. The fields cut at the shared rate and recover at **half** it (`GlideCap` `Racer.cs:1665`), the brake side mirrors them (`UpdateBrakeReasonCaps` `Racer.cs:1745`, `ComposeBrakeCap` `Racer.cs:1795`), and the composed `MaxThrottle`/`MaxBrake` are no longer drawn. Overrides (grid wait, countersteer, stuck recovery, offtrack) re-tag after.
- **The overspeed reason is the incline-boost anti-cheat**: it compares measured forward Gs against the **total** wheel-pushed Gs with a deadband (`OverspeedExcessGs` `Racer.cs:3581`), so it fires whenever the car accelerates harder than its wheels are asking. It arms only on a straight line above its speed gate (`Racer.cs:1677`), which deliberately leaves the launch alone — the engine's assist there is exactly the excess it would otherwise read.
- **Off-track projection cap (`Racer.cs:1214`)** caps the combined input by how far off-centre the projection lands, ramping across the whole half-width to a shallow floor. It is applied as a `Min`, so it never cuts braking — but a negative cap **is** a brake command over the plan's own throttle.
- **The throttle/brake avoidance override was REMOVED.** **Asymmetry is by design** — corner is the loose target, route the tight one, so don't "balance" them with flat offsets; on high-grip cars the route term wins.

## Corner lifecycle (apex table + three held targets)
1. **Apex table at generation** — `BuildApexTable` (`TrackLoader.cs:231`) detects corner regions on the smoothed radius and `AddCornerRegion` takes each region's **smoothed minimum** as its apex, keeping the region's smallest precise radius alongside it as the 1g speed's radius; two apexes the car reaches close in time are one complex, and so is a pair whose exit and entrance aim the same way inside a short window — that branch catches a fast kink the car clears before it can react to the slow corner behind it, so the window must stay under the time a U-turn's two apexes take to be planned separately, or the second absorbs the first and the merged span never covers the lead-in. It keeps the **slower by smoothed radius** and re-walks the survivor's span. **Both span ends derive from the apex**, walked outward to where the smoothed radius opens past a multiple of the apex's own — entrance tighter than exit so the two ends cannot chatter — bounded by the region the detector already found and by a node cap that stays a node count, because a radius multiple there gave a sweeper twice its own radius. **The apex is the smoothed minimum, not the region's tightest node** — a ±4-node chord measures a wide corner below the file's own decimal quantum. A region must also run a minimum length and carry a tight enough apex to register at all, which is what drops the blips and shallow bends; no radius flag survives, and the racer decides per corner (`UpdateCornerRequirements` `Racer.cs:2840`).
2. **Held apex queue per racer** — `UpdateApexLeapfrog` (`Racer.cs:2797`) prunes stale entries and leapfrogs passed ones each tick, while `RefillApexQueue` (`Racer.cs:2883`) **derives the queue fresh on every refill**: slot 1 is always the nearest corner ahead, the rest the most restrictive corners this car wants a braking plan for; the queue invalidates on a route-data change or a low-speed incident. Per-car apex speed is physics, `√(grip · gravity · radius)`.
3. **Braking map** — every held apex the car has flagged for braking feeds a kinematic solve over the usable distance and `cornerSpd` takes the lowest demand (`Racer.cs:1477`); its decel is grip-limited times a **per-apex learned factor**, seeded per racer per corner and corrected from the share of the braking phase spent at full pedal, with a flat cut for a corner taken with a real slide (`CommitBrakeLearning` `Racer.cs:1361`). The buffer is a flat time × speed, pressure does **not** scale it, and **a car whose ABS holds the pedal down reads as under-braking and drifts its factor up** — the learner watches pedal share, not achieved decel.
4. **Route-speed split** — route speed governs sweeping corners while the three-apex map handles tight ones from far away, and **min governs** (`Racer.cs:1544`). **Braking anchors on the corner entrance less a flat lead** (`BrakeTargetBaseNode`), which retires the turn-in lead that scaled with speed and radius — the same node the release and the learned factor's sampler use, so plan, release and learning all name one node — and the apex plan is released only once braking is genuinely done, down at apex speed (`Racer.cs:1493`).
5. The old live rolling and far-killer scans are **superseded**, but `Brain.Corner` is **live again**: `CommitApexQueue` (`Racer.cs:2975`) re-instances it each tick from the nearest held apex, so the corner line, the commit lane and the maneuver gates read live state.

Track facts: node counts are treated as metres; measured spacing on `Airport Quick 1` averaged 1.034 m/node over 1491 gaps (0.877–2.394 m), so the convention is count-as-metres, not exact spacing. Circuit lookaheads use modulo and point-to-point clamps.

## Instability — the throttle cut's landing state (moved out of the project memory, verbatim)

- **Instability owns the throttle cut now, and it is a first cut (`Racer.cs:1675`)**: the 3 Hz `WheelsOnGround` latch, `AirborneThrottleLevel` and `MaxThrottleFromStability` are **removed**, and `IsUnstable` reads on every **timed core** pass, never per frame (`AutosportRacingSystem.cs:1888` paces the batch), firing when the chassis rides above the at-rest height captured in `Launch` or when its yaw rate demands more lateral acceleration than the known grip allows. `CurrentMechanicalGrip` still carries no stability factor, and nothing models roll, pitch, load or suspension.

## Durable gotchas — steering and pipeline (moved out of the project memory, verbatim)

**Trigger**: steering trap, pipeline trap, clamp, NaN, steer sign, limiter, gravity, pursuit, "do not fix this".

- **The pursuit's steer saturates at the bearing it clamps to, and there is no separate "recovery law" to preserve** — `PursuitSteerFromBearing` (`Racer.cs`) is the `atan` of the pursuit's own sine term, so past the clamp the command would fold back down instead of flattening, and any saturation or crossover angle is a derivation off that form rather than a value the code holds. The `ARS.Clamp` NaN trap is already the NaN discipline bullet in `AGENTS-TECHNOTES.md`, and the line once cited for a "recovery law" (`Racer.cs:731`) is `Math.Sign` of the corner direction inside `ComputeHighSpeedLane` — neither a clamp nor a recovery term.
- **Offroad gravity needs a baseline reset before the multiplier** — `Initialize` (`Racer.cs:417`) runs every race and on respawn, so setting gravity immediately before the offroad multiply is load-bearing or it stacks across restarts.
- **Do not go hunting for an old limiter that used the static TRlat — the ceiling never reads it.** `Handling.LateralTractionCurve` is only the *input* to the speed-scaled peak (`LateralPeakAtSpeed`, `Racer.cs:1023`) plus the countersteer gate thresholds, the slide blend and the TCS spin map, and every limiter function (`AckermannCeilingDegrees`, `GeometrySteerCeiling`, `PeakSlipCeilingAt`, `ResolveSteerCeiling`, `ApplySteerLimits`) has a caller.
- **A POSITIVE steer command steers LEFT — do not re-derive this.** `Racer.cs`'s own lane law gives it away (`laneSteerDeg = -laneErrorMeters * laneGain` against the `+ = right` **lane** convention, which is a different convention), the rival repulsion and corner-commit lane agree, and `TrackPoint.Angle` is already in the steer's convention (a left-hand corner is positive), so a reference built from it must be used as-is. `SteerLimitLeft` bounds positive commands and `SteerLimitRight` negative ones, which is what `Racer.cs`'s clamp now reads (`92d5867`), while the Show Inputs fan draws each limit on its true side — orange left, red right. A rename here is a *side* swap, not a symbol swap, so check every use by side rather than by name.
